Learn Fine-tuning and Small Language Models with Phoebe · Session 2 of 6

Small language models: what you give up, what you get back

A small model is not a big model with the quality turned down. It is a different bargain: fewer parameters, so less room for facts and less general range, in exchange for a weight file you can hold on one modest GPU, run inside your own account, and tune for one narrow job. Tonight we read three real model cards the way a builder should, field by field: parameters, context, licence, memory, and the one sentence on a card that tells you which job to give the model.

Part 1 · covers parameters and context

What "small" buys, and what it costs

5 min live

"Small" here means roughly 3 to 4 billion parameters: Meta's Llama 3.2 3B Instruct at 3.21B, Microsoft's Phi-4-mini-instruct at 3.8B, and Qwen3-4B at 4.0B. Parameters are where a model stores what it learned in pretraining, so fewer of them means less room for facts. The second number on every card is context length: how much text the model can read in one request. Read the two together, and read the footnotes, because the context figure can change with the variant you actually deploy.

LiveWhat the parameter count buys3 min▶

A parameter count is a capacity budget. Pretraining spends it on grammar, reasoning patterns, style and a great many facts. Shrink the budget and something has to give, and the thing that gives first is breadth of factual recall. That is not a rumour about small models: Microsoft's own card for Phi-4-mini-instruct says the model "simply does not have the capacity to store too much factual knowledge", and suggests pairing it with a search engine.

For a fine-tuning project that is good news, not bad. Session 1 already sent facts to retrieval and behaviour to the weights. A small model is a reasonable home for a narrow behaviour, such as putting a claim note into one of six queues, precisely because that job needs consistency, not encyclopaedic recall. If the job needs facts, the retrieval side is taught in Learn RAG with Phoebe ↗.

LiveContext length, and the footnotes that move it3 min▶

All three cards quote generous context lengths, but read how each one gets there. Llama 3.2 3B Instruct supports 128k, and its quantized variants support 8k. Qwen3-4B reads 32,768 tokens natively and 131,072 with YaRN, a technique for stretching the position encoding beyond the native window. Phi-4-mini-instruct lists 128K. Three different stories behind three numbers that look alike.

The working question is not "which is longest?" but "how long is my input, in the variant I will actually serve?". A Larkline claim note with its instructions is a few hundred tokens (constructed). Any of the three fits with room to spare, even Llama's 8k quantized variant. A contract-review task would be a different conversation.

Self-studyThe other fields worth copying down2 min▶

Beyond size and context, each card carries details that matter once you deploy. Llama 3.2's card lists 8 officially supported languages and a pretraining data cutoff of December 2023. Phi-4-mini-instruct lists 23 languages, a data cutoff of June 2024 and a vocabulary of 200,064 tokens. Qwen3-4B's card describes 36 layers, grouped-query attention with 32 query heads and 8 key-value heads, and a thinking mode that is on by default, with a non-thinking mode available.

That last detail has a cost consequence: a model that reasons out loud before answering produces more output tokens per request. For a one-label routing answer you would test the non-thinking mode, and measure the difference on your own eval rather than assume it.

Real world

A card that tells you the job. Phi-4-mini-instruct is a 3.8-billion-parameter model whose own card warns about its factual capacity and points you to search. That one sentence is worth more than any leaderboard row when you choose a base model: the people who trained it are telling you to give it behaviour from the weights and facts from retrieval.

Part 2 · covers licences

Open weights are not open source

5 min live

All three models let you download the weights. That makes them open-weight models. Whether they are open source is a separate question, answered by the licence. Qwen3-4B ships under Apache 2.0 and Phi-4-mini-instruct under MIT, both licences approved by the Open Source Initiative. Llama 3.2 ships under the Llama 3.2 Community License, Meta's own licence: open weights, but not an OSI open-source licence. Which one you hold decides who in your company has to read what before you ship.

LiveThe vocabulary, said precisely3 min▶

Open weights means the trained parameters are published and you can download and run them. It says nothing about what you may do next. Open source, in the sense the Open Source Initiative defines, is a property of the licence: the licence must allow anyone to use, study, modify and redistribute the work, without discriminating against a field of use. Apache 2.0 and MIT are on the OSI's approved list. The Llama 3.2 Community License is not; Meta wrote it, and its clauses are its own.

The practical consequence is about effort, not good and bad. A team using an Apache 2.0 or MIT model can lean on licences its legal team has read many times before. A team using a custom licence has to read that licence, for that model version, against its own use. Both are normal; only one is quick.

Self-studyThree questions to take to the licence text2 min▶

Write your use in one sentence first, then ask the licence three things. May we use the model in a commercial product or service? May we modify it, and a fine-tune is a modification? May we redistribute the weights, ours or the original, to anyone outside the company? A tuned adapter you ship to customers is a different case from a model that runs only inside your own account.

Read the licence text, not a summary of it, including a summary on this page. Licences are versioned, and a new model release can come with new terms. This is not legal advice: the answer that counts is the one your legal team signs.

Real world

The licence is named on the card, not implied by the download button. All three cards read on 2026-11-08 name their licence explicitly: Llama 3.2 Community License, Apache 2.0, MIT. Constructed Larkline example: Priya sends Larkline's legal team the three names and a one-line use, "internal claim routing, the model never leaves our cloud account, no weights redistributed". Legal answers the Apache and MIT pair the same afternoon and books a reading of the Llama licence for the following week. Nobody said no; one path simply took longer.

Part 3 · covers quantisation, edge and latency

Bytes per parameter, and where the model can run

5 min live

Every parameter is a number, and every number takes bytes. At 16-bit precision each parameter takes 2 bytes, at 8-bit 1 byte, at 4-bit half a byte. Multiply by the parameter count and you have the weights-only memory: about 8 GB for a 4.0B model at 16-bit, about 2 GB at 4-bit. That one multiplication decides which GPU you can rent, and whether a laptop is even worth trying. It is arithmetic, not a measurement, and it is only the weights.

LiveQuantisation: what is stored, and what you trade3 min▶

Quantisation stores each weight with fewer bits. The memory saving is exact and easy to compute; the quality cost is not, and it has to be measured on your own task. Model makers work hard to keep that cost small. Meta's card for Llama 3.2 describes its quantized variants: 4-bit groupwise weights with a group size of 32, produced with SpinQuant or GPTQ, plus a variant trained with quantisation in the loop (QAT) and LoRA. Groupwise means each block of 32 weights shares its own scale, so one outlier does not distort the whole matrix.

Notice what the same card says next: those quantized variants support 8k of context, not 128k. Quantisation is a package deal, and the card is where the package is described. Session 4 meets 4-bit weights again from the training side, when QLoRA tunes on top of them.

LiveLatency is something you measure, not something you quote2 min▶

Fewer parameters and fewer bytes usually mean faster responses, but "usually" is not a number. Latency depends on your GPU, your serving stack, your batch size, your input and output lengths, and for Qwen3-4B on whether thinking mode is on. None of the three cards gives you a latency figure for your setup, and a number copied from a blog post was measured on someone else's.

The professional move is to state the requirement as a number someone signed up to (session 1: urgent claims flagged within 5 seconds, constructed) and then time the candidate on hardware you would really use, at a realistic load. Session 6 treats latency as an input you supply, for exactly this reason.

Self-studyEdge, on-device and data residency2 min▶

A 2 GB weight file opens doors an 8 GB one does not: a single small GPU, and sometimes a laptop or another device at the edge, where the data never travels to a server at all. Whether it runs fast enough there is, again, a measurement. For reference, Hugging Face lists an L4 with 24 GB of GPU memory at $0.80 an hour on its Inference Endpoints (AWS; list price read 2026-11-08, re-verify), and every bar in the figure above sits well under that line.

Constructed Larkline example: Larkline's security policy says claim notes may not leave its own cloud account. That one sentence rules out sending notes to a third-party API, and points at an open-weight model served inside Larkline's account, whatever the per-request arithmetic says. The price there comes from Larkline's own cloud provider; the L4 line is a sense of scale, not a quote.

Real world

The card describes the quantized model as a different product. Meta did not just say "also available in 4-bit". The Llama 3.2 card names the method (SpinQuant, GPTQ, and a QAT plus LoRA variant), the scheme (4-bit groupwise, group 32) and the changed limit (8k context). When you pick a quantized build, read its own row of the card, and evaluate that build, not the full-precision one it came from.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · You can download Llama 3.2 3B's weights from Hugging Face. Which statement is accurate?

Open weights describes what was published; open source is a property of the licence. The card names the Llama 3.2 Community License, Meta's own licence. Apache 2.0 belongs to Qwen3-4B and MIT to Phi-4-mini. Internal use is still use: D skips the reading.

2 · Qwen3-4B has 4.0B parameters. Roughly how much memory do its weights alone take at 4-bit?

4-bit is half a byte per parameter: 4.0B x 0.5 = 2.0 GB. B is the 16-bit figure (2 bytes per parameter) and C is 8-bit. The serving footprint is larger than the weights, so the answer always carries "weights only".

3 · Phi-4-mini-instruct's card says it "simply does not have the capacity to store too much factual knowledge". What does that suggest for a build?

The card itself suggests pairing the model with search. That matches session 1's split: tune for behaviour, retrieve for knowledge. Quantising shrinks the stored numbers; it does not add capacity, so D gets it backwards.
