Learn Fine-tuning and Small Language Models with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

Prompt, RAG or fine-tune? The decision ladder

"Should we fine-tune?" usually arrives as a solution looking for a problem. Fine-tuning is a real tool with a narrow job: it changes how a model responds, the format, the tone, the boundary of one task, and it does that by changing the weights once, at training time. It is not how a model learns this week's facts. Tonight we place each lever where it actually acts, climb the five-rung ladder from prompt to distillation, and score one real-shaped task against it before anyone rents a GPU.

Part 1 · covers what fine-tuning changes

Where each lever acts

5 min live

Every lever on the ladder works in one of two places. Prompts, worked examples and retrieved documents all go into the context window, so they change what the model sees on this one request, and they can change again on the next. A fine-tune changes the weights, so it changes how the model responds to everything, once, at training time. Most bad fine-tuning projects are a fact problem sent to the weights.

LiveBehaviour, format, style, one narrow task3 min▶

What a fine-tune is good at is consistency of response. The same six queue names spelled exactly the same way every time. A JSON object with no chatty preamble. A house tone. A task boundary, such as "route, never answer the customer". These are things a prompt can ask for and a model can still drift away from, especially a small model, and they are things that a few hundred good demonstrations can pin down.

OpenAI's own supervised fine-tuning guide puts the order bluntly: "Only invest in fine-tuning after setting up evals." That one line is the shape of this whole path: the eval comes first, the tune second, and session 5 is where the two meet.

LiveFresh facts: the disagreement, stated fairly3 min▶

"Fine-tune it on our documents so it knows our policies" is the most common request, and the evidence leans hard against it. Ovadia and colleagues (2023) compared unsupervised fine-tuning with retrieval for injecting knowledge and found that retrieval consistently outperformed fine-tuning, for knowledge the model had seen and for entirely new knowledge. Gekhman and colleagues (2024) found that examples carrying new facts are learned significantly slower than examples consistent with what the model knows, and that once learned they linearly increase the model's tendency to hallucinate. The LIMA paper (Zhou et al. 2023) argues that almost all knowledge is learned during pretraining.

The other side deserves its due. Ovadia's paper also reports that training on many paraphrases of the same fact may ease the difficulty, and a narrow vocabulary, such as Larkline's internal queue names, is "knowledge" a tune does teach well. The working rule most practitioners use is: fine-tune for behaviour, retrieve for knowledge. Treat it as a strong default, not a law, and test it on your own eval. The RAG side of that rule is taught in Learn RAG with Phoebe ↗, which uses the same split.

Real world

The model card that says it out loud. Microsoft's model card for Phi-4-mini-instruct, a 3.8-billion-parameter model, says the model "simply does not have the capacity to store too much factual knowledge" and suggests pairing it with a search engine. The people who built the small model are telling you which job to give it: behaviour from the weights, facts from retrieval.

Part 2 · covers the ladder

Five rungs, climbed in order

5 min live

The ladder runs from the cheapest, most reversible lever to the most expensive and most locked-in: a prompt, then worked examples in the prompt, then retrieval, then a fine-tune, then distillation into a smaller model. Each rung costs more time, more data and more ownership than the one below. You climb a rung only when the one below has been tried and has failed on your eval, not on a hunch.

LiveRungs 1 to 3: the ones you already own3 min▶

Prompt. Clear instructions, the six queue names with a one-line definition each, and the output format. Minutes to change, free to undo. Few-shot. Three to five worked examples in the prompt: hard-to-describe boundaries are easy to demonstrate. Still no training. Both are taught properly in Learn Prompt Engineering with Phoebe ↗. RAG. Retrieve the documents the answer depends on and put them in the context. It is the rung for knowledge that changes, and it lives next door.

For Larkline the first two rungs are already in place: the routing prompt has instructions and four worked examples. Retrieval adds little, because routing a note to a queue does not depend on facts that change weekly. That is already a hint about which rung is next.

Self-studyRungs 4 and 5: fine-tune, then distil3 min▶

Fine-tune. Train the model's weights, usually a small adapter on top of frozen weights (session 4), on a few hundred to a few thousand labelled demonstrations of the exact behaviour. You now own a dataset, a training run, an eval, and somewhere to serve the result. Distil. Use a big model as the teacher: it labels or explains thousands of examples, and a much smaller student is trained on them. Hsieh and colleagues (2023) report a fine-tuned 770-million-parameter T5 that outperformed few-shot prompted 540-billion-parameter PaLM on one benchmark, using 80 percent of the available data, by training on the teacher's rationales as well as its labels.

Read that result with its limits: one benchmark, one task family, in the authors' own setup. It shows what is possible on a narrow task, not that small beats big in general.

Real world

The rung can move under you. OpenAI's supervised fine-tuning guide, read on 2026-11-08, says the platform "is no longer accessible to new users", and that eligible existing customers can create training jobs until January 6, 2027. A team whose plan was "tune it on the vendor's API" now needs a different plan. What survives a vendor change is the labelled dataset and the eval, which is why sessions 3 and 5 treat those as the real assets.

Part 3 · covers when to climb

Three questions before rung four

5 min live

Three questions do most of the deciding. Is the gap about facts that change? Then retrieval, not a tune. Do you have an eval that shows the prompt has stopped improving? If not, the eval comes first. Only then: is the task stable, high-volume or latency-bound enough that a tuned small model would pay for itself? Session 6 puts numbers on that last question.

LiveLong prompts against a tune: the second disagreement3 min▶

Context windows grew, and with them a real argument: why tune at all when you can paste hundreds of examples into the prompt? Bertsch and colleagues (2024) found that for datasets with large label spaces, in-context performance keeps rising with thousands of demonstrations, and that fine-tuning is more data hungry than in-context learning, but can exceed it with additional data. The LoRA Land report (Zhao et al. 2024) went the other way on its own benchmark set: across 31 tasks, 4-bit LoRA fine-tuned models outperformed GPT-4 by 10 points on average.

Both can be true, because they measure different tasks under different budgets. Long prompts cost you on every single request; a tune costs you once and then on hosting. Which wins for you depends on your volume, your latency and your eval, and the honest move is to test both, not to quote one paper.

Self-studyThe third question, in words before numbers2 min▶

A tune pays for itself when three things hold at once. The task is stable: the six queues do not change every quarter, so the labelled set stays true. The volume is high: a per-request price paid hundreds of thousands of times a month is larger than a fixed hosting bill. Or the latency matters: a small model on your own GPU can answer faster than a large one over an API. If none of the three holds, the cheaper rungs win on total cost even if the tune wins on accuracy.

There is a fourth, quieter reason that sometimes decides alone: data residency. Some notes may not leave Larkline's own cloud account. That is a constraint, not a cost, and it points at an open-weight small model regardless of the arithmetic.

Build-along

Score Larkline's task against the ladder

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Constructed Larkline example. Priya answers six questions about the claim-triage task, one line each, and marks whether the answer points towards a tune or away from one. Write the same six lines for a task of your own as you go. If you have none, use Larkline, and notice which line stops the project.

Name the gap in one line, then ask which side of Part 1's figure it sits on. If the gap is "it does not know X", stop and look at retrieval. If the gap is "it does not do X consistently", keep going.

★ Step 1 · behaviour or knowledgeGap: the big model routes well, but costs too much at 900,000 notes a month and is slow at peak. Side: behaviour. Six fixed queue names, one label per note. No fact in the answer changes week to week.

Check that the task will hold still. A tune learns the task as it is on the day the data was labelled. Ask the owner how often the categories have changed.

★ Step 2 · stabilityAsked Tom (claims operations lead, constructed): "When did the six queues last change?" Answer: two years ago, when Fraud and loss was split out. Next planned change: none on the roadmap.

Ask for the eval, by name. Not "we check it sometimes": a fixed set of labelled examples the current system is scored on, kept out of any training. If it does not exist, this line says HOLD.

★ Step 3 · the evalCurrent eval: none. Quality is judged by complaints. Action: label 600 held-out notes before any tuning (session 3 builds it; session 5 scores against it). Status: HOLD

Write the volume and the current cost per request. Rough is fine tonight; session 6 makes it exact. The question is only whether volume is large enough to matter.

★ Step 4 · volumeVolume: ~900,000 routed messages a month (constructed) Per request: ~600 input tokens (prompt + 4 examples + note) ~20 output tokens (queue + urgency) Verdict: large enough that the per-request price matters

State the latency need as a number someone signed up to. "Fast" is not a requirement. "Urgent claims flagged within 5 seconds of arrival" is.

★ Step 5 · latencyRequirement: urgent queue flagged within 5 seconds (constructed) Today: meets it off-peak, misses it at Monday 09:00 peaks Points to: a model Larkline can scale itself, or a faster API tier

Find the labels you already have, and say how much you trust them. Most teams have history: what a human did with each item. It is a start, not a gold set.

★ Step 6 · dataHave: 18 months of notes with the queue an agent chose Trust: unchecked; agents re-route ~1 in 20 later (constructed) Next: relabel a sample and measure agreement (session 3) Verdict: enough to try a tune, after cleaning

Read the scorecard top to bottom, then act on the first HOLD. Priya's Monday is not "start the fine-tune". It is "label 600 held-out notes and score the current prompt on them". That set is what will later prove or disprove the tune, and it is useful even if the answer is "keep the big model".

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Larkline's refund policy changes every quarter, and agents want the model to quote the current one. Which rung fits?

A changing fact belongs in the context window, where it can change too. Ovadia et al. (2023) found retrieval consistently beat unsupervised fine-tuning for knowledge, and Gekhman et al. (2024) found new facts are learned slowly and raise hallucination once learned. D pastes in stale versions as well as the current one.

2 · Which statement about fine-tuning and evals matches OpenAI's own supervised fine-tuning guide?

The guide says it in those words. Without an eval made before the tune, there is no baseline to beat and no way to tell a better model from a different one. B builds the eval from the thing it is supposed to judge.

3 · A colleague cites LoRA Land: "tuned small models beat GPT-4 by 10 points, so we should tune." What is the fair reading?

LoRA Land (Zhao et al. 2024) reports a 10-point average over its own 31 tasks. Bertsch et al. (2024) find long-context in-context learning keeps improving with thousands of demonstrations. Both are task-specific; your eval decides for your task.
