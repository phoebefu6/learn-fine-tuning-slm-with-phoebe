Part 1 · covers the eval scorer

Four numbers, and the one that hides

4 min live

The scorer compares each row's true queue with the queue an arm routed it to. Every number comes out of one table: the confusion matrix, true queue down the side, routed queue across the top. The diagonal is what went right. A row of the matrix divided by its total is that queue's recall, the share of its messages that reached the right desk.

LiveAccuracy says tune; the urgent queue says wait2 min▶

On the 600 rows the tuned small model routes 566 correctly, 94.3 percent, against 546 for the big model with a prompt, 91.0 percent. Macro-F1, which weights the six queues equally, agrees: 0.936 against 0.910. On those numbers alone the tune wins. Now read the Fraud and loss queue: the big model catches 98.2 percent of it and the tuned model 84.2 percent. The tune got better at the common queues and worse at the rare, urgent one, which is exactly the shape a model trained on imbalanced history tends to take.

The predictions behind these numbers are modelled, so the specific values are a teaching case, not a measurement of any product. The lesson is the shape: a headline metric can rise while the one queue that carries the risk falls, and only a per-class view shows it. Session 5 built that view; here it becomes a floor.

Self-studyThe third arm: the small model before the tune2 min▶

The bench also scores the same small model with the prompt only: 74.2 percent accuracy, macro-F1 0.736, with Payments and fees its weakest queue at 63.5 percent. The gap from 74.2 to 94.3 is the tune's contribution, and the gap from 74.2 to 91.0 is what the big model's size buys without any tuning. Comparing a tuned small model only against the big one hides the first gap; comparing it only against its own untuned self hides the second. Show both.

Score the arms, then find the break-even

Then work the five moves in the tip below, in order, and say out loud what changed.

Five moves, in order. (1) Read the default: GO, $1,084 a month against $1,224, break-even at 797,304 requests. (2) Tick "Retrain every month": the flat line rises to $1,688 and break-even moves to 1,241,422, so the answer becomes NO-GO. Untick it. Part 2 · covers break-even and serving

A sloped line against a flat one

4 min live

The big API model is billed per token, so its monthly cost is a straight line through zero: double the requests, double the bill. The tuned small model is billed mostly per hour of GPU, so its cost is close to flat until one GPU runs out of capacity. Break-even is where the two lines cross. Below it the API is cheaper; above it the tune is.

LiveWhat is inside the flat line2 min▶

Three things, and the training run is the smallest. Hosting: one NVIDIA L4 (24 GB) on Hugging Face Inference Endpoints lists at $0.80 an hour, so 730 hours is $584 a month. The one-off build, the labelling, eval and integration work, is set at $6,000 (constructed; put your own in) and amortised over 12 months, $500 a month. The training run itself: 2,000 examples of 150 tokens for 3 epochs is 900,000 tokens, which at Together AI's $0.34 per million supervised fine-tuning price is $0.31, so the $4.00 minimum charge applies. Hosting and people are the cost. GPUs for training, at this size, are a rounding error.

On the other side, the big model's line is $1.36 per 1,000 requests at gpt-4.1's list price of $2.00 per million input tokens and $8.00 per million output. Most of that is input: Larkline's prompt carries instructions and four worked examples on every single call. A tuned model needs neither, which is one of the quiet savings a tune buys.

LiveThree ways to serve a tuned model2 min▶

A dedicated GPU you rent by the hour. Flat cost, your own capacity, the shape the bench models. Hugging Face lists an L4 at $0.80, an L40S (48 GB) at $1.80, an A100 (80 GB) at $2.50 and an H100 (80 GB) at $4.50 an hour; Together AI lists a dedicated H100 at $5.49. Serverless per-token hosting. Some providers serve small open models per token, Together lists Qwen3.5 9B at $0.17 input and $0.25 output per million, which turns the flat line back into a shallow slope; check whether your provider serves your own adapter on that price. The vendor's own fine-tuning API. No GPUs to run, but OpenAI's platform, the best-known one, is closed to new users, with jobs for existing customers until January 6, 2027.

All of these prices were read on 2026-11-08 and change often. Re-verify before any number leaves the room.

Real world

The rival nobody priced. On the bench, switching the big model from gpt-4.1 to gpt-5-mini, whose list price is $0.25 per million input tokens and $2.00 per million output, drops the API bill to $171 a month and moves break-even to 5,707,018 requests. A smaller API model is often the cheapest rung no one tested. The bench cannot tell you its accuracy, because it only models one big arm; your held-out set can, in an afternoon, which is the cheapest experiment in this whole decision.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever, the break button and drift

Two ways to fool yourself, and the one that arrives later

4 min live

The go/no-go rule has two conditions joined by AND: the tuned model must clear the accuracy floor, and it must cost less at your volume. "Cheapest wins" drops the first condition, and it is the version that tends to survive a budget meeting. Retraining is the cost most first plans leave out. Drift is the reason you will need it.

LiveThe anti-lever, with its honest nuance2 min▶

Set the floor on fraud and loss recall at 92 percent and the bench prints NO-GO: 84.2 percent does not clear it. Switch to "Cheapest wins" and the same inputs print GO, with a warning that the floor would refuse. The same happens with a 96 percent floor on overall accuracy, which 94.3 does not clear. Cheapest-wins never improves a decision; at best it agrees with the floor, and when it disagrees it is the one that is wrong about risk.

The nuance: the floor is only as good as the number it is set at. A floor nobody can justify ("why 92?") will be argued down in the meeting. Set it from the cost of a miss, a fraud claim routed to a slow queue, and write that reasoning in the memo.

LiveThe break button: retrain every month2 min▶

A tuned model is frozen on the day its data was labelled. If Larkline's notes drift, a new product line, a storm season, a new fraud pattern, the tune ages while the big API model is updated by its vendor. Plans that include a monthly retrain usually forget its cost. Tick the box: one more training run ($4.00) plus $600 of people time each month (constructed: relabelling, re-running the eval, redeploying). The flat line rises from $1,084 to $1,688, break-even moves from 797,304 to 1,241,422 requests, and Larkline's 900,000 is now on the wrong side.

That does not mean "never retrain". It means the retrain cadence is a number in the business case, chosen from how fast the eval decays, not from habit.

Write the decision as one line someone could disagree with. Not "explore fine-tuning". A decision with a date.

★ Line 1 · decisionDo NOT switch Larkline's claim triage to the tuned small model this quarter. Fix the urgent queue first; re-run the bench by the end of next month.

Report the eval with the urgent queue beside the headline. Copy the numbers the bench printed, and say that the predictions are modelled when they are.

Give cost at your volume and the break-even, with the prices dated.

★ Line 3 · costAt 900,000 requests a month: big model $1,224, tuned small $1,084 ($584 GPU, $500 build amortised). Break-even 797,304. List prices read 2026-11-08; build cost is our estimate.

Name what would flip the answer, in both directions. This is the line that makes the memo honest.

★ Line 4 · flip conditionsFlips to GO if: Fraud and loss recall reaches 92% on the same held-out set, with no other queue falling below 90%. Flips harder to NO-GO if: we must retrain monthly (break-even 1,241,422 > our 900,000), or gpt-5-mini matches the big model's accuracy ($171 a month).

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the bench's defaults, the tuned small model scores 94.3% accuracy against the big model's 91.0%, but 84.2% recall on Fraud and loss against 98.2%. What does a floor of 92% on fraud and loss recall do?

The rule is AND: clear the floor, then be cheaper. 84.2 is under 92, so the verdict is NO-GO before cost is read. C is exactly what "cheapest wins" prints, and the bench flags it as a GO the floor would refuse.

2 · Larkline adds a monthly retrain: one $4.00 training run plus $600 of people time. What happens to break-even?

The compute is small; the people time is not. Every fixed monthly cost raises the flat line and pushes the crossing to a higher volume. The bench's break button shows the flip from GO to NO-GO.

3 · Where does most of the tuned small model's monthly cost come from at Larkline's defaults?

900,000 training tokens at $0.34 per million is $0.31, so the $4.00 minimum applies. One L4 at $0.80 an hour for 730 hours is $584, and $6,000 of build work over 12 months is $500. Hosting and people are the cost.
