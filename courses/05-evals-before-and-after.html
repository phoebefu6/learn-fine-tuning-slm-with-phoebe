Evals before and after the tune

A tuned model is not better because its training loss went down. It is better if it beats the thing it would replace, on the same frozen set of examples it never saw, on every part of the task that matters, without breaking anything it used to do. Tonight we score three arms on 600 real customer messages, read past the headline accuracy to the queue that carries the risk, and add the regression set that catches what a tune forgets.

Part 1 · covers the held-out set and the baseline

One frozen set, three arms, one scorer

5 min live

An eval compares like with like. The held-out set is frozen before any tuning and never enters training. The baseline is the system you would replace, the big model with its prompt, scored on that same set by the same scorer. The small model with the prompt only is the third arm: it shows how much of the gain the tune itself bought.

LiveScore the baseline before you tune3 min▶

The baseline is scored first, on the frozen set, before a single training step. Two reasons. It tells you the bar: the big model with its prompt routes 546 of the 600 rows correctly, 91.0 percent, so a tune that reaches 90 percent is a step backwards, however good 90 sounds. And it removes the temptation to choose the eval after seeing the tuned model's results, which is the eval equivalent of drawing the target around the arrow.

How to build the frozen set properly, a golden set with coverage of the hard cases, is taught in Learn AI Evals, the golden set ↗. Here it is enough that it was labelled by people, frozen, and kept out of training.

LiveThe third arm says what the tune bought2 min▶

The small model with the prompt alone reaches 74.2 percent. After the tune, 94.3 percent. That 20-point gap is the tune's contribution; the gap between 74.2 and the big model's 91.0 is what size buys without any tuning. Report all three. A tuned-against-big comparison alone hides how weak the starting point was; a tuned-against-untuned comparison alone hides whether you beat what you would replace.

Real world

The eval that was in the training data. Banking77, the public dataset behind these rows, has 7 test texts that also appear verbatim in its training split (session 3 found them by normalising case and spacing). A model trained on the full training split has seen those 7 answers. On a well-known public benchmark the effect is small; on a hand-built company set, where the same customer message is often logged twice, it can be most of your "improvement". Check overlap before you trust any after-number.

Part 2 · covers per-class metrics and the confusion matrix

Read the queue that carries the risk

5 min live

Accuracy is one number for six queues of different sizes and different stakes. Recall per queue asks how many of that queue's messages reached the right desk. Macro-F1 averages each queue's balance of recall and precision with equal weight, so a small queue counts as much as a big one. On the bench, the tuned model wins on accuracy and macro-F1 and loses on the urgent queue.

LiveRecall, precision, macro-F1 in one minute each3 min▶

Recall for a queue: of the messages that belong there, the share that got there. Read it as "how many urgent claims did we catch". Precision for a queue: of the messages routed there, the share that belonged. Read it as "how much of the urgent desk's time goes on non-urgent work". Macro-F1: for each queue, F1 balances the two; macro averages the six with equal weight. On the bench the tuned model's macro-F1 is 0.936 against the big model's 0.910, and the untuned small model's 0.736.

Each metric answers a different operational question, so decide which one guards each queue before the tune runs. For Larkline, the urgent queue is guarded by recall, because a missed fraud claim costs far more than a few extra messages on the urgent desk.

Self-studyWhy a tune can make the rare queue worse3 min▶

A tune learns from the history it is given, and history is imbalanced. In Banking77's training split the six queues carry 860, 2,237, 1,581, 2,336, 1,976 and 1,013 rows, with Fraud and loss the smallest. The bench's predictions are modelled, but they are modelled on this familiar shape: the tuned arm improves the big queues and slips on the small urgent one. The fixes are on the data side, more and better urgent examples, and on the decision side, a recall floor for that queue that the tune must clear.

Routing is an exact-match task, so no judge model is needed to score it. When an output is free text, a summary or a reply, the scorer itself becomes a model, and calibrating it is its own discipline: Learn AI Evals, LLM-as-judge ↗.

Part 3 · covers catastrophic forgetting and the regression set

What the tune might forget

5 min live

A tune optimises the task in its training data. Anything else the model did, and that you relied on without writing it down, can drift: the output format, a refusal, a language, a rule. That loss of earlier behaviour is called catastrophic forgetting, and the defence is a regression set, a fixed list of behaviours the tuned model must still show, scored on every candidate.

LiveForgetting, and what LoRA does about it3 min▶

Biderman and colleagues (2024), in a paper titled "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less", compared LoRA with full fine-tuning on code and maths. In standard low-rank settings LoRA substantially underperformed full fine-tuning on the target domain, and better maintained the base model's performance on tasks outside it. That is a trade, not a free lunch: the adapter's small, low-rank update (session 4) learns less of the new task and disturbs less of the old.

Neither result removes the need to measure. A LoRA tune forgets less on average, in their settings; your regression set says whether it forgot the one thing your system relies on.

Self-studyBuilding the regression set3 min▶

Start from the incidents and the unwritten rules. Every behaviour someone complained about once, every format a downstream system parses, every refusal compliance asked for, becomes a small fixed set of inputs with an expected property. Keep it separate from the held-out set, version it, and run it on the base model too, so a drop is measured against where you started. Suites, versioning and running them in CI are taught in Learn AI Evals, regression suites ↗.

Real world

The same rule from the people who sell fine-tuning. OpenAI's supervised fine-tuning guide says "Only invest in fine-tuning after setting up evals", and advises carving the holdout set from the same collected data with roughly the same diversity of inputs and responses. The vendor's own guidance puts the eval before the training job, which is the order this session follows.

Build-along

The before-and-after table

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Constructed Larkline example, real rows. Open the bench in session 6 in a second tab, Part A only, and build the table Priya hands to Tom: three arms down the side, six queues across, recall in every cell, and a pass condition for each queue written before you look.

Write the pass conditions before you open the bench. One line per queue, chosen from what a miss costs. Doing this first is what keeps the eval honest.

★ Step 1 · pass conditions (constructed)Fraud and loss recall at least 92% (a missed claim is the costly error) Every other queue recall at least 90% Overall at least the baseline's accuracy Regression set no kept behaviour drops

Score the baseline. In Part A, pick "Big API model + prompt". Copy accuracy, macro-F1 and the six recall values from the recall bars into the first row.

★ Step 2 · baseline row, as the bench prints itBig API model + prompt Accuracy 91.0% (546 of 600) Macro-F1 0.910 Recall: Fraud 98.2 Cards 92.9 Transfers 93.3 Top-ups 88.2 Payments 81.7 Account 97.3

Score the starting point and the candidate. Repeat for "Small model, prompt only" and "Small model, LoRA-tuned". Watch the Fraud and loss bar and the first row of the confusion matrix as you switch.

★ Step 3 · two more rowsSmall, prompt only 74.2% (445) Macro-F1 0.736 Small, LoRA-tuned 94.3% (566) Macro-F1 0.936 Tuned Fraud and loss recall: 84.2% (48 of 57)

Check each queue against its pass condition, not against the other arm. The question is not "is the tune better?" but "does the tune meet the bar we set?".

★ Step 4 · the checkFraud and loss 84.2 < 92 FAIL Cards 97.9 Transfers 93.3 Top-ups 97.1 Payments 94.2 Account 91.8 all >= 90 pass Overall 94.3 >= 91.0 pass Verdict: not yet. One queue fails its floor.

Reproduce the numbers in code on your own set. The bench computes the same metrics in your browser; on your own data, scikit-learn does it in three lines. Read the code; your numbers will differ.

★ Step 5 · the same metrics in Pythonfrom sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, f1_score, classification_report # y_true: the frozen held-out labels; y_pred: one arm's routed queues print(accuracy_score(y_true, y_pred)) print(f1_score(y_true, y_pred, average="macro")) print(classification_report(y_true, y_pred, digits=3)) # per-queue recall and precision

Write what goes back to the data. A failed queue is a data task before it is a model task: more and cleaner examples of that queue, then a new tune scored on the same frozen set and the regression set.

★ Step 6 · next iterationAdd: 400 relabelled Fraud and loss notes from the last 18 months Keep: the 600-row held-out set and the regression set unchanged Re-run: tune, then all three arms' rows again Hand to session 6: the cost side of the same decision

Never move the held-out set between runs. The moment you add the hard urgent notes to the held-out set after seeing the failure, the before and after numbers stop being comparable. New hard cases go into a new, versioned set, and both are reported.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · When should the big-model baseline be scored?

Like with like: the same frozen rows and the same scorer, set before you see the tuned model's results. A chooses the eval after seeing the answer; B measures a different task; C includes rows the tuned model trained on.

2 · The tuned model scores 94.3% accuracy against the baseline's 91.0%, and 84.2% recall on Fraud and loss against 98.2%. What is the right reading?

Accuracy averages six queues of different sizes and stakes. The urgent queue's recall answers "how many urgent claims did we catch", and 48 of 57 is 9 urgent messages sent to slow desks. The pass condition, written first, decides.

3 · What does a regression set catch that the held-out routing set does not?

Catastrophic forgetting is loss outside the target task. Biderman et al. (2024) found LoRA forgets less than full fine-tuning on average in their settings; a regression set measures whether your tune forgot the thing your system needs.
